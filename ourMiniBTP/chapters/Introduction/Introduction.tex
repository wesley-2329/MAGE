\section{Introduction}\label{Chapter-1-Introduction}
\noindent The rapid advancement and democratization of Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed the landscape of digital communication, content creation, and software development. Modern architectures such as GPT-3/4 \cite{brown2020language}, LLaMA \cite{touvron2023llama}, and Claude can generate highly coherent, contextually relevant, and stylistically sophisticated text that is often indistinguishable from human writing. Built upon the foundational attention-based Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}, these models have scaled up to hundreds of billions of parameters, enabling emergent capabilities. While these technologies enhance productivity across numerous domains, they also present significant security, ethical, and societal risks. Automated systems can be exploited to generate deceptive product reviews, scale up misinformation campaigns, automate phishing attacks, and facilitate academic plagiarism. Detecting machine-generated text has therefore emerged as a critical requirement for maintaining trust and security in digital ecosystems.

\section{Background on AI-Generated Text Detection}\label{Chapter-1-Background}
\noindent Early efforts in AI-generated text detection relied primarily on lexical and statistical properties. Traditional methods analyzed token distributions, word frequencies, and n-gram occurrences to find patterns unique to language models. However, as generative architectures transitioned from statistical n-gram models to deep neural networks, the stylistic gap between human and machine text narrowed. Modern LLMs are trained on massive datasets using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and instruction-tuning. Consequently, static lexical detectors are no longer sufficient, leading to the development of deep contextual classifiers (like BERT \cite{devlin2018bert} or DistilBERT \cite{sanh2019distilbert}) and zero-shot statistical estimators.

\section{The Generalization Gap and Closed-Set Limits}\label{Chapter-1-Generalization-Gap}
\noindent Despite the high accuracy of modern supervised classifiers (often exceeding $95\%$ in controlled environments), they suffer from a severe generalization gap when deployed in the wild. As demonstrated in out-of-distribution (OOD) baseline evaluations \cite{hendrycks2016baseline}, deep models struggle under domain and generator shifts. 

Supervised classifiers are traditionally framed as closed-set systems. In a closed-set setup, the model is trained on a predefined set of classes and is forced to map every input to one of those classes. For example, a closed-set generator attributor trained to classify text as either GPT or LLaMA will be forced to choose one of these two options when presented with text generated by OPT \cite{zhang2022opt}. This leads to high-confidence misclassifications and compromises the reliability of forensic audits.

\section{Motivation for Open-Set Rejection}\label{Chapter-1-Motivation}
\noindent To address the limitations of closed-set systems, there is a critical need to implement an explicit open-set generator rejection layer. An open-set framework models the boundary of known distributions and introduces an \texttt{UNKNOWN} class. When the system encounters text from an unseen generator, it evaluates the distance of the sample's representation to the known generator distributions. If the distance exceeds a validation-tuned threshold, the text is flagged as \texttt{UNKNOWN} rather than being incorrectly mapped to a known generator family. Developing such a pipeline is essential for engineering resilient forensics systems capable of operating under unconstrained, real-world conditions.

\section{Research Objectives}\label{Chapter-1-Objectives}
\noindent The principal objectives of this study are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item To reproduce a manageable, multi-domain text detection benchmark utilizing the dataset from the ACL 2024 MAGE paper \cite{li-etal-2024-mage}.
    \item To implement and evaluate multiple text detection paradigms, including lexical n-grams (FastText \cite{joulin2016bag}), probability rank predictability (GLTR \cite{gehrmann2019gltr}), zero-shot likelihood curvature (DetectGPT \cite{mitchell2023detectgpt}), and supervised transformers (DistilBERT \cite{sanh2019distilbert}).
    \item To build an explicit open-set generator rejection layer using class centroids, covariance profiling, and Mahalanobis distance \cite{mahalanobis1936generalized}.
    \item To evaluate the OOD rejection pipeline under controlled pooling (representation) and distance metric studies.
    \item To stress-test the generalization capacity of the open-set rejection layer using a leave-one-generator-out validation scheme across GPT, LLaMA, and OPT families.
\end{itemize}

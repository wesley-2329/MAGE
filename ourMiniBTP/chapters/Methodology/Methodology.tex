\section{Dataset Description and Source}\label{Chapter-2-Dataset}
\noindent The baseline dataset for this research is derived from the official MAGE benchmark (\texttt{yaful/MAGE}), compiled for the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL 2024) study \cite{li-etal-2024-mage}. The complete MAGE dataset serves as a multi-angle benchmark covering 10 writing domains and 27 LLM variants. To accommodate local hardware constraints while preserving the scientific integrity of the OOD evaluation, we extract a balanced subsample of three writing domains and three major generator families.

\subsection{Orthogonality of Domain and Generator}
In text forensics, writing style (domain) and generator characteristics (model family) represent orthogonal dimensions. A single generator family (e.g., GPT \cite{brown2020language}) can generate text across multiple domains (news summaries, forum posts, and creative stories). Similarly, a single domain can contain text generated by multiple different model families. The detector must learn to distinguish the stylistic footprint of the generator independent of the semantic topic of the domain.

\subsection{Selected Writing Domains}
The three domains chosen for this study represent highly divergent writing styles and vocabulary distributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{XSum (Extreme Summarization):} Comprises formal, factual news summaries. The language is structured, concise, and objective, with a heavy emphasis on factual reporting and formal syntax.
    \item \textbf{ELI5 (Explain Like I'm Five):} Consists of informal, explanatory forum answers. The text is conversational, highly explanatory, and uses simpler vocabulary and sentence structures designed to clarify complex concepts.
    \item \textbf{WritingPrompts:} Comprises creative fiction stories. The text is narrative, highly descriptive, uses diverse vocabulary, and features complex sentence structures, metaphors, and creative dialogue.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Generator Families}
We focus on three major generator families that represent different structural and pre-training designs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GPT Family:} Proprietary, closed-source models aligned using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and extensive instruction-tuning (e.g. GPT-3.5-turbo \cite{brown2020language}).
    \item \textbf{LLaMA Family:} Open-weights transformer architectures trained on large public corpora using standard autoregressive self-supervised objectives (LLaMA-7B/13B \cite{touvron2023llama}).
    \item \textbf{OPT Family:} Open Pre-trained Transformer models developed by Meta, sharing architectural similarities with LLaMA but trained on a different corpus distribution (OPT-13B/30B \cite{zhang2022opt}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Strict Data Partitions and Leakage Prevention}\label{Chapter-2-Splits}
\noindent A critical requirement of open-set validation is the absolute prevention of data leakage. As formulated in OOD detection literatures \cite{hendrycks2016baseline}, if the unseen generator (e.g., OPT) is exposed to the model during training, validation, or parameter tuning, the decision boundaries will adapt to it, rendering the OOD evaluation invalid. 

We implement a leave-one-generator-out validation scheme. For each of the three configurations, the held-out generator is completely excluded from the training and validation sets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Experiment 3A (OPT Unseen):} Classifier is trained and validated strictly on GPT and LLaMA. The OPT family is held out and loaded exclusively during the final testing phase.
    \item \textbf{Experiment 3B (LLaMA Unseen):} Classifier is trained and validated strictly on GPT and OPT. The LLaMA family is held out.
    \item \textbf{Experiment 3C (GPT Unseen):} Classifier is trained and validated strictly on LLaMA and OPT. The GPT family is held out.
\end{itemize}

\section{Feature Extraction and Contextual Representation}\label{Chapter-2-Features}
\noindent We utilize the pre-trained \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased} model \cite{sanh2019distilbert} for extracting contextual embeddings. The raw text passages are processed through the DistilBERT tokenizer, converting strings into sequences of token IDs. The sequences are padded or truncated to a fixed maximum length of 512 tokens.

During the forward pass of DistilBERT, the token sequence is processed through 6 transformer layers, utilizing self-attention mechanisms \cite{vaswani2017attention} to construct bidirectional contextual representations. The representation of the first token (the special \texttt{[CLS]} token) in the final layer is extracted as the sequence embedding:
$$\mathbf{z} = \text{last\_hidden\_state}[:, 0, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$$
Because the \texttt{[CLS]} token interacts with every other token in the sequence through self-attention, it aggregates the semantic and syntactic context of the entire text passage, providing a rich 768-dimensional representation.

For classification, $\mathbf{z}$ is passed to the classification head:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A pre-classifier linear layer that projects the representation ($768 \to 768$) with a ReLU activation function.
    \item A dropout layer ($p = 0.2$) to mitigate overfitting.
    \item A classifier linear layer ($768 \to N$) that maps the representation to class logits.
\end{enumerate}
This architecture is fine-tuned end-to-end on the training splits.

\section{System Architecture}\label{Chapter-2-Architecture}
\noindent The overall architecture of the MAGE-based AI text detection and open-set rejection framework is presented in Figure 2.1. The system consists of two sequential stages: Stage 1 performs baseline detection of AI-generated text using parallel FastText, GLTR, and DistilBERT classifiers. If the text is classified as AI-generated by the DistilBERT branch, it enters Stage 2 for open-set rejection. This stage computes the Mahalanobis distance from the CLS embedding of the input text to the reference centroids and covariance statistics constructed offline from known training classes, and rejects the text as an unknown generator if the distance exceeds the precomputed 95th-percentile OOD threshold.
